# Targeted Therapy for Cancer

## Overview

Targeted therapy is a precision medicine approach that treats cancer by targeting specific proteins controlling cancer cell growth, division, and spread. This foundation of precision medicine allows researchers to design treatments based on DNA changes driving individual cancers.

## How targeted therapy works

Targeted therapies interfere with specific proteins that help tumors grow and spread, differing fundamentally from chemotherapy which kills all rapidly dividing cells. Mechanisms include: helping the immune system destroy cancer cells, stopping growth signals, preventing blood vessel formation, delivering cell-killing substances directly to cancer cells, triggering programmed cell death, and starving cancer of required hormones.

## Small molecule inhibitors

Small-molecule drugs are small enough to enter cells easily, so they are used for targets that are inside cells. These oral medications (pills or capsules) target intracellular signaling pathways and are administered at home or in clinical settings based on cancer type and treatment protocol.

## Monoclonal antibodies

Monoclonal antibodies are proteins produced in the lab designed to attach to specific cancer cell targets. Functions include marking cancer cells for immune destruction, directly stopping growth, triggering self-destruction, or carrying toxins to cancer cells. These are typically administered intravenously through needle injection into blood veins.

## Antibody-drug conjugates

Some monoclonal antibodies combine with cell-killing substances including toxins, chemotherapy drugs, or radiation. Once attached to cancer cell targets, cells internalize these conjugates and die, while untargeted cells remain unharmed. This represents a hybrid approach between antibody and chemotherapy mechanisms.

## Biomarker testing requirement

For some types of cancer, such as chronic myelogenous leukemia (CML), most people with that cancer will have a target for a certain drug. However, most of the time the tumor will need to be tested to see if it contains targets for which there is a drug. Biomarker testing may require biopsy, which carries varying risks depending on tumor size and location.

## Side effects

When targeted therapy was developed, scientists initially believed it would be less toxic than chemotherapy. They have learned that targeted therapy can also cause serious side effects. Common effects include diarrhea and liver problems. Other potential side effects include blood clotting issues, high blood pressure, fatigue, mouth sores, nail changes, hair color loss, and skin problems. Rarely, perforation through organ walls may occur. Most side effects resolve after treatment ends, and medications can prevent or manage many effects.

## Limitations and resistance

Targeted therapy has notable drawbacks: cancer cells can develop resistance when targets change or cancer cells discover alternative growth pathways independent of the target. Additionally, drugs for some targets are hard to develop due to structural or functional complexities. Because of resistance, targeted therapy may work best when used with more than one type of targeted therapy or with other cancer treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiation.
