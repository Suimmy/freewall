# Cushing's Syndrome (High Cortisol)

## Overview

Cushing's syndrome is a rare endocrine disorder where the body produces excessive cortisol over prolonged periods. Endogenous Cushing's affects approximately 40-70 people per million, most commonly adults aged 30-50, with three times more women affected than men.

## What cortisol does

Cortisol, called the "stress hormone," maintains blood pressure, regulates blood glucose, reduces inflammation, and converts food into energy. The adrenal glands — small organs atop the kidneys — produce this hormone under pituitary direction.

## Symptoms of high cortisol

People with sustained elevated cortisol display characteristic signs: weight gain, thin arms and legs, a round face, increased fat at the neck base, and a fatty hump between the shoulders. Additional manifestations include easy bruising, purple stretch marks, and weakened muscles. Women may experience excess facial and body hair; men may have reduced fertility.

## How high cortisol affects body weight

Cushing's syndrome causes distinctive fat redistribution — not general weight gain. The pattern includes centralized accumulation, facial fullness, and dorsal neck prominence alongside thin extremities. This metabolic pattern reflects the specific pathophysiology of cortisol excess and is a diagnosed endocrine condition fundamentally different from typical weight gain caused by caloric imbalance.

## Causes of elevated cortisol

The most common cause is prolonged glucocorticoid medication use for asthma, rheumatoid arthritis, or lupus. Endogenous Cushing's develops when tumors drive excess cortisol: pituitary adenomas (80% of cases), ectopic ACTH-producing tumors (often in lungs), or adrenal tumors producing cortisol directly.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires two confirmatory tests among: 24-hour urinary free-cortisol measurement, late-night salivary cortisol testing, or low-dose dexamethasone suppression testing. Cushing's syndrome can be hard to diagnose because symptoms overlap with polycystic ovary syndrome and metabolic syndrome, necessitating careful medical evaluation.

## Treatment

Treatment depends on cause. Pituitary tumors typically require surgical removal with success rates as high as 90 percent when done by a highly experienced surgeon. Ectopic and adrenal tumors require surgical removal; medication may reduce cortisol levels. Glucocorticoid-induced cases involve gradual dosage reduction or medication substitution under medical supervision.
